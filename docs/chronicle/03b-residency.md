# 15–31 October — keeping the right bytes near the GPU

## Experiments leading to FE2

Shadow buffers moved GPU-written data into video memory. Already on the first day, `24903ad` narrowed write-back to the words a dispatch changed. As more of the game rendered, shader specialization, texture uploads and the number of times recording had to stop became costs in their own right. `122f21d` introduced a fast specialization hash with a comparison check. `e327ebc` kept render passes open when a draw sampled a depth aspect that it did not write. Both changes exploited information the renderer already had about what a draw would consume.

Residency work began binding read-only inputs from a persistent device mirror (`1b38599`, 15 October). Experiments challenged intuitions about where the time went. On 21 October buffer device addresses raised measured GPU cost by 16% in the recorded experiment (`a3d0873`). On 24 October doubling a shadow limit from 4 to 8 MiB added 14 ms of GPU time per frame, so the smaller default survived. At the sampled 1-1 locations the project's own GPU work occupied 29–38% of a frame (`068e214`). Speeding up shaders alone could not recover the entire interval. Another experiment computed the next frame's starting register state from current command buffers in 4,159 of 4,161 frames (`7c90a19`). The exceptions mattered for any plan to record ahead.

## 15–16 October — three different reasons to preserve an allocation

The memory investigations were converging on three distinct identity questions. The guest expected a physical allocation to retain its contents when mapped again elsewhere. Vulkan expected an imported host address range to stay usable until in-flight work finished. The renderer wanted its faster video-memory copy to survive into the next draw. Each could outlive the other two. Satisfying one lifetime by extending all three would consume ever more memory. Ending all three on a guest unmap had already lost the device.

The experimental physical carry path in `f3b1f83` parked nonzero pages at unmap and restored them by physical offset. The title performed about 27,000 remaps totaling 8 GB per tour. That is a substantial amount of work to reproduce something the console gets naturally by remapping the same physical storage. The section path replaced the underlying model with views. Commit `2adcdf7` records an immediate secondary consequence: kernel-mode I/O sometimes had to be retried across view splits. The caller could regard a destination as contiguous while the host had separate mappings inside it. The later Linux staging and short-read tests are another instance of the same problem at the boundary between guest addressing and the host kernel.

The first residency stages could use the sparse mirror for read-only ranges while leaving harder writers on earlier paths (`1b38599`). A physical graphics card is not required to allocate memory covering the mirror's whole virtual span. Sparse binding attaches device backing as ranges become active. This keeps address arithmetic simple for the shader while allowing the host to provision finite VRAM. It creates a second kind of missing page that matters to diagnostics. A valid guest mapping can lack current mirror backing. A sparse allocation can also remain backed while its contents are obsolete after a CPU write. Allocation, mapping and validity have to be represented separately.

A different resource was held too long at the display end. In `c6fd568` a flip packet began reserving its place in VideoOut's queue immediately, then becoming presentable after earlier GPU work completed. Until then the buffer still counted as busy for the game's safe-to-render check. This let the graphics CP continue without exposing unfinished pixels. Both the gate mean and the play median improved about 1.9%, with a clean eight-level tour. A tempting simpler change, just returning success immediately, would have freed the game to overwrite the queued buffer. Reserving now and committing later is still visible in today's VideoOut implementation.

## 17–21 October — the right place to spend a microsecond

Grouping three or more lane-local vector instructions under one execution guard reduced SPIR-V words by 22% on a 5-2 frame and cold compile time by 11% (`c83c8c0`). It was legal to reuse a register value within such a block because the instructions touched only the lane's own registers. An optimization with similar-looking syntax would be unsafe across a wave operation. One has to know which state is private and which state may have changed for the group.

A separate case on 19 October connected colourful streaks on the screen to a register spill. The game used lane-addressed LDS instructions to save and restore temporary registers in a pixel shader. The host translation only provided ordinary LDS in compute. Writes were being dropped and reads retained stale register values. Between 57 and 195 such warnings appeared per run. The flock in 1-2 produced negative colours that bloomed into cyan. A private array per invocation preserved those spills in stages without LDS. The 2,051 modules in the 1-2 capture validated, the streaks disappeared, and an eight-level tour matched apart from one unreproduced black frame in 4-3 (`822908f`). It is a useful predecessor to the Linux fog work. A fault visible as a colour can originate in an instruction far removed from shading the final surface.

CPU recording costs also changed with allocation policy and thread placement. Allowing batch slots to grow segments as needed reduced their gate footprint from 5.5 to 1.2 GB (`d7b1b5a`). Keeping front-end threads on one last-level-cache group gained about 6% in the reported experiment (`59680f8`). But simply dedicating a physical core to the graphics CP later cost 4.3%. The expensive lock in `06ba061` guarded a 112 MB home for culling indices. Checking its roughly 28,000 pages cost about 126 microseconds and blocked other workers that wanted the same home. The game could cause hundreds of epochs per frame. Using title submission or REWIND boundaries for these checks cut checks by 35% and worker waits for the lock by 70%, for 2.4% more flips/s at the gate. Work avoided while holding a shared resource can benefit several threads at once.

Consecutive small dispatches of the same program were also candidates for batching. The default in `fc392ab` reduced CPU time per command by 4–5%. A draw census in `cc9eae4` found only 23% of draws differed solely in their buffers. Much of the setup remained sensitive to image bindings or other state. The experimental mirror mode in `99b661e` copied a written range at the start of a batch and gathered changed words at the end. That reduced the scope of writes back to guest memory, but comparing two versions adds GPU work of its own. Exact shader-generated marks later provided more direct evidence of what to copy.

## 21–24 October — snapshots, replay and the limits of prediction

A display-mode switch exposed a stale image that stayed on screen after the title had registered a different buffer size. Looking up the image by the registered size fixed that case (`593c120`). Another switch caused a newly created G-buffer, a set of intermediate images used in deferred shading, to lose three layers. The draw had to consider every colour slot binding the same image to infer its layer count (`a2f1763`). The programmer could have fixed a screenshot by preferring the largest cached image. Neither bug was about which cached object looked most plausible. Both were about the identity and full extent specified by the current guest state.

Could the next frame be recorded while the current one was still rendering? `7c90a19` found that a state walk computed the next start state exactly for 4,159 of 4,161 frames. Even a correct register prediction was only half the answer. The probe in `b035dd3` found the first necessary flush after only about 37% of the next frame. A speculative recorder needs the right *memory contents* at each point it reads draw arguments and descriptors. It can be certain about the register naming an address and still be too early to read that address.

Within a submission the idea did find a narrower use. `dd725af` let the main CP keep recording after handing a run to parallel workers. It accumulated deferred work, then checked the workers' actual end state, the absence of conflicting CPU reads, and whether any image had pending clears or reloads. If the checks failed it could drop the stretch and record it again. The gate comparison improved about 0.8%. Extending the scheme into the next submission lost 2.8% and was removed. Recoverability was part of the design: the project even exposed a mode that forced every stretch to drop to exercise the fallback.

Profiling provided a counterweight to adding complexity. `1ab0163` showed that keeping staging buffers pooled could make the driver refuse guest memory imports. Unpooling them brought failure counts back to the baseline, but a warm walk gained no speed. Quiet-machine comparisons of GPU-side loads, speculative work and async uploads clustered between 20.4 and 21.0 fps at the tower vista. `555e75b` added hitch attribution and fresh-session boss runs. All sixteen developers' fog-door entries ended in victory with no error lines. Such a campaign exercises very different content from a stationary vista, though it bypasses the progression that an ordinary player uses to reach a boss.

## 26–31 October — FE2 wins a moving comparison

On 26 October `7b3087c` found the vista gained only 3.4% in flip rate at 1920×1080, saving about 1.2 ms of GPU time. Its queue accounting included 13.8 ms of graphics, 10.6 ms on queue 0x50, 5.2 ms on workers' chunks and 5.5 ms for other compute queues. Reducing pixel count had left substantial geometry, compute and command costs intact. On 27 October eliminating tessellation-pass copies saved 1.1–1.6 ms on the device, yet the next visible dependency was a graphics CP wait for queue 0x50. Frame time did not follow the copy savings (`9ff531c`).

Queue 0x50 issued roughly 880 particle programs at 25–100 microseconds apiece. The first command of batches cost about 13 ms of CPU per flip in aggregate (`42b7bac`). Thousands of small binding and setup decisions made an expensive frame without any single spectacularly slow shader. FE2 put buffers in one sparse GPU address space and made freshness a page-level question. If a page had stayed clean, another upload was unnecessary. Finding that out could itself take longer than the copy it avoided.

On 27 October `a66e92a` replaced global disarming after bursts of writes with local backoff. A burst could suspend arming in one 64 MiB chunk while other chunks retained their clean pages. It also repaired a crash. A range-wide protection change could fail across decommitted pages, but the old bookkeeping cleared every armed bit anyway. A neighbouring page then remained read-only with no record explaining the fault. Disarming in mapped runs and clearing only confirmed bits restored agreement. At the gate, takes fell from 1,784 to 59 per flip and their time from 23.9 to 1.9 ms. Even so, FE2 delivered about 21.7 flips/s against about 25.4 for the earlier default in that comparison. A faster component had yet to produce a faster whole frame.

On 29 October waits that released nothing new stopped advancing the epoch (`785e787`). Independent 64 MiB write-watched allocations reduced contention within a host reservation (`f259f81`). A command-buffer census found no whole buffer repeating a recent frame's, limiting simple replay (`07734fc`). Moving imports off recording threads on 31 October showed no measured gain (`e9211fc`). The decisive FE2 comparison involved a moving camera. At the vista it reached 25.0–25.4 flips/s against 24.0–24.2 on the earlier path. GPU busy time and the 99th-percentile frame both improved. Yet on a 59.94 Hz walk the old FE2 build's area median was 22.6 against 24.7. A moving window saw about 3,400 takes per flip and 121 ms of aggregate thread time. Advancing a worker's required view whenever *any* queue changed epoch made the renderer repeatedly prove things the worker could not yet observe.

In `6941c1b` the graphics CP advanced its view at its own synchronization boundaries and acquired the writer's view when passing a label from another queue. Its parallel workers inherited the planning view. One moving window rose from 20.95 to 24.75 flips/s. Worker checks fell from 68.7 to 33.2 ms of aggregate time per flip. Across the whole walk the area median became 23.9 against 23.4 on the earlier front end, but Firebridge remained behind at 15.8 against 20.6. The same report found 93% of takes empty and substantial waits on the import-list lock. Averages could now support the design without explaining every slow area.

The precise decision came in `e07a683` on 30 October. FE2 became the development default, the self-hosted Windows configuration became normal for it, and the old front end remained available as a fallback without an obligation to keep it fast. The release default was still a later decision. On 31 October the culling index outputs alone helped explain about 40 MB of write-back per flip (`c5ec6d7`). With one mirror established, the [next problem](03c-coherence.md) was how to retain its benefits across writers, queues and synchronization points.
