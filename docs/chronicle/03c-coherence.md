# 1–10 November — overlapping queues and proving visibility

On 1 November ordered asynchronous compute let dispatches use a compute-only queue. Upload prologues ran on the main queue. Timelines carried progress between queues, and consumers waited where there was an actual dependency, including indirect arguments and promised labels. The swamp gained 9.2% in an in-boot comparison, Gatehouse Hall 3.8%, and the swamp's worst frames fell from 50–54 to 42 ms (`acf869b`). Removing write marks on the assumption that selected mappings were safe had already fed GPU-driven work stale data. That experiment was removed (`4fef704`).

## 1–2 November — reducing the cost of an arrival

Some improvements mattered most at the instant a level streamed in. The image cache could hold its lock while deciding on re-uploads and performing expensive layout conversion. That made all other users of the cache wait on a large texture. Moving the choice and detiling of re-uploads out from under the lock brought Castlewall arrival's 99th-percentile frame from 69.6 to 48.8 ms across whole boots. Accumulated hitch time dropped from 640 to 347 ms, while mean throughput rose only 2.8% (`f99c538`). For someone walking into the area, the tail of the distribution better describes the visible change.

On the same day thirty experimental switches were closed in `3321c3b`. Fourteen candidates and their code were removed and sixteen measured defaults became unconditional behavior. Roughly 4,100 lines disappeared. The removed experiments included frame-ahead recording, GPU data-load variants and batch draws. Among the survivors were cost-based parallel splits, the lighter end-of-pipe barrier, off-thread reset and lane guards. A subsequent reader who sees only today's code could reasonably wonder why the obvious alternative was never tried. Here the absence of a switch is the result of testing it, not evidence that it was overlooked. The vista stayed level and tours remained clean after consolidation.

Asynchronous compute gave two device queue families work that could overlap (`acf869b`). The benefit depended on where a label, an argument or an upload actually required a wait. A microbenchmark in that commit ran barrier chains divided between queues in 0.48–0.58 times their single-queue duration. The same commit's full-scene improvements were considerably smaller because real consumers did depend on earlier producers. A second queue also changes the meaning of “done.” Even a batch whose own fence is complete may still sit behind an earlier batch's deferred *CPU* actions. Splitting slot release from ordered publication removed an unnecessary connection between these two lifetimes. Later moving scenes exposed dependencies the implementation did not yet cover, and by 14 November single-device-queue execution was again the default (`487e262`).

At Castlewall `0e1d420` counted roughly 49 MB a flip in 152 write-backs, taking 5.4 ms of GPU time, about a fifth of the measured total. Every covered mapping was marked CPU-readable. The guest's declared permissions therefore provided no basis for omitting any of them. Meanwhile the graphics CP was waiting for 27% of its time, 16% for the GPU and 11% for workers. Speeding up every instruction on that thread could touch only its busy share. This set up the next question: could the runtime know when the CPU *actually* read a GPU-produced page?

## 3–6 November — evidence of a write, evidence of an observer

A shader's touched-page bit exists to get a page onto a list for later write-back. Hundreds of stores to a page do not require hundreds of list entries. Checking the bit before issuing an atomic retained the single append and reduced contention (`3b3126d`). Marking individual words still mattered. A single page can hold adjacent records with different writers. The executor cannot infer from a single dirty-page bit which words should overwrite guest memory. This separates two levels of bookkeeping one might otherwise collapse: the list of pages to visit and the masks of words to transfer on each visit.

A store that first checked whether its page was already marked could avoid a redundant atomic operation. That saved 1.28 ms of GPU busy time per swamp frame on 3 November (`3b3126d`). A different redundancy test failed across queues. If graphics previously wrote value A, compute then wrote B, and graphics returned to A, a graphics-only hash could incorrectly say guest memory already held A. Invalidating the other family's hash on store repaired that case (`1f22fa1`). CPU rewrites also threatened the premise of a hash skip, and unconditional page write-back became the default in `233922b`. These bugs were possible even when each shader computed the right answer.

An audit compared guest bytes with the mirror, taking care to exclude pages an unfinished batch was still writing. The hash fix in `1f22fa1` reduced several counts of stale GPU-written pages per boot but did not drive every sample to zero. Armed runs went from counts of 9/8/4/3 to 0/4/1 in the sets recorded. The CPU-only pages were exact. Arrival geometry at 8-2 could go wrong in about one in five isolated boots whether arming was enabled or not. The evidence justified the specific cross-family invalidation and another look at CPU rewrites. It did not justify assigning every bad vertex at 8-2 to page arming.

Another useful fast path was much less ambitious. A placement repeated within the *same* batch, epoch, queue view and mapping could use a direct-mapped table private to its executor. In `570659f` this applied to roughly half of placements, cutting CPU per command by 1.2% at Doran and 2% at the vista, with 1.9% more frames at the vista. An eight-level tour remained clean. The narrow cache key and short lifetime made the correctness condition explicit. Later work asked a broader question about ranges sharing pages, which required additional validation.

On 6 November a GPU-side wait replaced about 0.5–0.7 ms per frame of graphics-processor thread waiting before submission. The gain was 1.0% at Doran and 0.7% at the vista (`e6132de`).

These days also changed services a renderer profile would miss. In `2af23b3` a loud static and occasional one-ear sound led to splitting the audio signal by source. AudioOut2 port type 6 carried a DualSense haptic waveform with a 53 Hz peak and bursts around hits and rolls. Mixed as sound it dominated the speakers and drove the mix into compression. That channel was routed to pad motors. The title's ambisonic sound field, which describes sound arriving from directions, was decoded for headphone, stereo and multichannel output. The Windows spatial-object experiment had corrupted the heap and stayed off. The Linux SDL backend later inherited a game whose audio channels already had identified purposes.

The same commit illustrates how the PC menu stays connected to the running game. A console variable's constructor held a storage pointer at offset +0x60. For rendering variables the destination was a field in a settings block the engine copied for each view. Changing only the field at +0x68 often changed nothing on screen. Writing the actual backing storage as well let still-camera comparisons distinguish controls with visible effects from settings that did nothing useful. Several candidates, including certain depth-of-field and screen-space reflection overrides, were left out. A menu full of effective controls required understanding where the engine *read* a setting, not just where the UI stored a choice.

## 7–10 November — which round trips can disappear?

Some frame work consisted solely of small synchronization events. A CP batch holding only labels and timestamps could become its own ordered part of the next parallel run instead of forcing a standalone submission. In `3be2dde` the gain was 0.6% at the vista and 0.8% at Doran with a clean validation run. Moving a much larger operation could fare worse. Sending write-back off the graphics queue saved 3.3 ms of measured GPU busy time at the swamp while frame throughput fell to 0.96 of baseline (`43832d4`). The next consumer needed the result. The time to deliver it, including waiting for another queue, mattered more than the sum of device work.

For pages that stayed wrong, the audit read through every covering import. `808bfd2` found the imports aliased the same bytes and the guest pages were read-only and armed. `b08ca44` then found neither queue still had write marks set and no write-back was pending on the sampled lasting page. This ruled out two simple readings of that observation: a disconnected CPU alias and an ordinary outstanding transfer. The recorded elimination is valuable even where the trace stops short of a complete cause. It directs attention to the device-side state and the history used to decide whether to write, rather than repeatedly changing how a host pointer is mapped.

The allocator itself offered a narrower target for delayed visibility. A diagnostic helper sampled writes to the game's GPU arena after submissions. In `9dce5a3`, 88–96% of dirty pages were within one pass of their last allocation or write. An all-buffer device-address alternative raised GPU busy time about 5% in that later experiment. Instead of changing the address model everywhere, C4 held selected pages and arranged to release them when the CPU touched them. The host coordinated holds with fault answers (`e4dc4c7`). One release had waited two seconds for a writer that in turn depended on the blocked thread. Commit `1d40ec9` bounded that wait, excluded GPU timestamp pages and made C4 the Windows default after same-view comparisons and a tour. The throughput ratios were 1.149 at the swamp and 1.015 at the vista. C4's stronger ownership contract and feedback into shader compilation both became important in the [following period](03d-recording.md).
